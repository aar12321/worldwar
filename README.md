# Worlds of Others

A browser-based, turn-based grand strategy game of total global hegemony. You command one of 175 nations; every other nation is run by an AI rival, or by a friend: one to four people can play the same world online. One turn is one month, starting January 2026.

## Quick start

```bash
npm install
npm run dev        # http://localhost:5173
npm test           # engine unit tests (Vitest)
npm run build      # type-check + production bundle
```

## How to play

The game has two modes, chosen when you start. In **Turns** (the default), one turn is one month, starting January 2026, and nothing moves until you press **Next turn** (or Enter). In **Live** (for one player), time flows by itself and you watch armies march: see [Live mode](#live-mode).

1. Pick a nation on the main menu (search the list or click the globe), and choose how skilled your rivals are. **You win by holding the whole world**: every country yours or a puppet of yours. It is a long game, built for about 12 to 15 hours of play. Taxes start at the neutral 20% (and business tax at 15%), so every change you make has an effect. **Easy** rivals play the basics, wait a long time before attacking you, and never band together against you. **Medium** rivals also grow cities, set budgets and laws, fund business, and form alliances and blocs. **Hard** rivals also borrow, invest abroad, sanction, counter the units you field, press an ally's enemy together, and turn a crushing win into a puppet state.
   **Scenarios.** Under *Scenario* on the main menu you can start somewhere other than the Standard world: a cold war between two blocs, a tiny country among giants, a peaceful builder's start, an arms race, a neighbor that collapses, a resource crunch, a plague year, a strait under fire, a rising superpower, a war on two fronts, a powder keg. Each has a plain-words briefing, its own goal (some win by surviving, growing, or out-earning; some use the usual goals) and a suggested nation and rival skill, which you can ignore. The Briefing desk shows the scenario's goal and how far along you are.
2. The game is run from **seven desks** on the left (Briefing, Economy, Military, Diplomacy, Faction, Empire, Research):
   - **Briefing** (`B`): your goal, a first-steps checklist for new players, and your advisor's list of what needs you this month. Every item has a button that takes you to the fix.
   - **Economy** (`E`): ten tabs (see [Statecraft](#statecraft) and [Caring for the people](#caring-for-the-people-and-building-for-the-future)): *People* is where you look after your country and *Projects* is where you build for its future. *Bank* is the central bank, *Budget* lists every money line, and *Government* has law slots. *Country* shows **your whole empire in one list** (every country you hold, its trait, how settled it is, and a **governor** who upgrades it each month on a plan you choose), then grows your cities (each takes a focus that boosts the whole country: industry, farming, harbor, research, fortress, finance) and buys Factory, Farm, University, Hospital, Barracks, Port and Civic Hall upgrades. *Budget* shows where money comes from and goes (every line, adding up to what is left over) and sets income tax and the draft; bonds, printing and interest rates are in *Bank*. *Market* buys and sells food, oil, steel and goods at world prices, within a monthly trade limit that ports raise, with auto-buy for shortages. *Stocks* is the exchange: made-up companies whose prices follow the economy, stability, taxes and troubles of their home countries, with dividends, crashes and rallies. You can only invest in companies in countries you hold, and a very developed country has no more than two. Buy shares to take a stake (10% investor, 25% board seat, 51% control, 75% dominant; big trades move the price against you). With a board seat you instruct the company: **pay dividends**, **expand the business** (its sector grows faster) or **specialize** (technology: research and cheaper Frontier systems; arms: cheaper units and upkeep; finance, energy, manufacturing and farming each lift the nation), at half strength with a board seat and full strength with control. Take a country in war and its companies, with your old owner's shares in them, are yours. *Business* has six private sectors that grow by themselves (set the business tax, give grants, subsidize, invest abroad). *Government* picks democracy, monarchy, junta or one-party rule and passes laws, within the room your empire has for them. *Space* runs your space program (see [The space program](#the-space-program)).
   - **Military** (`M`): six tabs, with *Missile War* beside *Wars* (see [The missile war](#the-missile-war)) (*Deployment* is described in [Active military and borders](#active-military-and-borders)). *Systems* runs the **missile program** and the Frontier programs (below). *Agencies* runs your intelligence service (the CIA in the USA) abroad and your security service (the FBI) at home: gather intelligence, build a case for war, sabotage, arm rebels, help a friend keep order, steal technology, back a coup on a shaky nation, and (with Cyber Warfare) cyber attack. Odds come from agency level, experience, technology, partners and the target's own security, and are shown before you commit. A noticed failure costs reputation and hands the target a reason to fight you. *Forces* trains infantry, tanks, artillery, fighters, bombers and warships (they arrive after 1 to 3 turns; vehicles cost steel and burn oil, and fighters decide how much bombers can do), buys permanent weapon levels and drill, and appoints a commander. Every unit shows its attack and defense and where each bonus comes from; battles use exactly those numbers. *Wars* (`W`) lists your wars (three at most), their monthly bills and each front's Defense Line.
   - **Diplomacy** (`D`): offers waiting on you, deals, and every neighbor's opinion of you with pacts, alliances, guarantees, trade, loans, investment, sanctions and peace terms, including making a defeated nation a puppet that pays tribute. A peace offer can **include the allies and puppets on both sides**, so one signing ends every war between them. The **Wars** tab lists each front weakest first with your odds, and *Make peace* opens the peace terms directly. Another nation's strength is a rounded **guess** until you are allied, at war, hold it as a puppet or have gathered intelligence on it.
   - **Faction** (`F`): countries that work like a company they own together (see [Factions](#factions)). Pool money, materials and soldiers, put a country to the vote, and fight as one army.
   - **Empire** (`I`): what your size opens up, how stretched your officials are, how you hold conquered land, your capitals and the World Council.
   - **Research** (`R`): spend research on technologies, in seven branches (the seventh is Medicine), and spare research on lasting programs. The **Frontier** branch holds the milestones: combat drones, cyber warfare, missile defense, artificial intelligence, hypersonic missiles, robotics, quantum computing, orbital systems and nanotechnology. Each unlocks something real, with a counter: **Combat Drones** (a unit that takes the first losses and shreds tanks and guns; shot down by fighters and shields), **Robot Infantry** (no soldiers needed, their losses never tire your people; hacked by a higher Cyber Command), **Hypersonic Missiles** (a missile kind that comes from technology and the training queue; see [Missiles](#missiles)), and six systems bought in three levels with upkeep: **Cyber Command**, **Interceptor Shield**, **AI Battle Command** (more attacks a month, exact odds and range, a stance recommendation, and at level 3 a planner that chooses your whole force and an auto-command; see [The War Room](#the-war-room-your-force-and-the-odds)), **Quantum Codebreaking** (warnings of where an enemy could strike), **Orbital Network** (intelligence on enemies, easier landings, one orbital strike a year) and **Nano-armor** (less damage, part of every loss comes back). The missile strikes are in the War Room (see [Missiles](#missiles)).
   - News (`N`), help (`?`) and settings sit in the top-left bar.
3. **War needs a reason.** A great power cannot just attack a neighbor. A reason comes from a border incident, an attack on your ally or a country you guarantee, a rival caught spying on you, sanctions against you, standing with a victim in a crisis, an operation by your intelligence service, or a **case** you build yourself (20 influence, ready in 3 months). With a reason, war costs 10 influence. Without one it costs 60, drops your stability, and the world distrusts you: every attack and captured country adds to your **reputation for aggression**, which makes other nations uneasy until it fades over a couple of years. Rivals follow the same rules.
   **The news** arrives in stages, never early: a rumor, a disturbance, then a crisis (unrest in a country, or one nation threatening another). You can send aid, mediate, or back the victim, or ignore it. Left alone it may boil over into uprisings or war, and allies may call you to join.
   **War is a campaign.** Click a neighboring country and press **Declare war**. You can attack across a border or a short sea lane; a port plus warships reaches any coast in the world, and fighters or strike aircraft with a factory as an airbase reach anything within 7000 km (these fight with a landing penalty). Then press **Plan an attack** (`A`) to open the War Room: choose **exactly how many of each unit** to send (a stepper for every kind, with None, Half, Probe, Main force and Max buttons, and how many missiles to fire with the attack) and how to fight (Assault, Blitz or Siege). A big **chance to win** updates with every click, with its range and what decides it (see [The War Room: your force and the odds](#the-war-room-your-force-and-the-odds)). The room also shows expected losses, what the force costs to keep and what the war costs. Each win pushes back the country's **Defense Line**; when it reaches zero the country falls. Big countries and capitals have long lines, small ones short. Lines heal a little in months nobody attacks them.
   - **Units you send are away until the turn ends**, so your home army fights at reduced strength and rivals can punish an all-in attack.
   - **Every war costs a monthly bill** that grows with your military and the enemy's size, and tires your people. A small country can be in three wars at once, and one more for every 12 countries it holds (up to eight). Your military can also launch more attacks a month as the empire grows: three, plus one for every six countries (up to nine). Training slots, influence and the number of unsettled countries you can hold also grow with the empire. A nation whose capital has fallen and whose army is gone, or under a fifth of yours, surrenders whole. A puppet fights beside you in every war, and after a year it can be **annexed** (15 influence for each of its countries): its land, army and treasury join yours. No nation costs more than 80 war score to make a puppet, however big.
   - **Every country has a trait** (chip foundries, financial hub, energy giant, rare minerals, breadbasket, mountain stronghold...) that helps your whole nation once it settles. When a nation falls its conqueror also takes 40% of its treasury and learns a technology it knew.
   - **Conquered land starts restless** and produces about 40% at first, settling over six months. Holding many unsettled countries strains stability.
4. **Next turn** runs the rivals, training, research, income, diplomacy, rebels and any attacks on you, replays the battles you were in, then shows a **monthly report**: what changed, the news, and what the advisor would do next.
5. The **map control** (bottom left) switches the globe between Nations, Relations (allies, pacts, enemies), Fronts (how much Defense Line is left) and Borders (how tense every border is; see [Active military and borders](#active-military-and-borders)).

## Statecraft

Beyond war, you now rule a country's money, people and industry, and what you can do grows with your empire.

- **Empire tiers.** Holding 4, 10, 25 and 60 countries (a puppet counts half, more when loyal and less when restive, in the tiers, the Countries Taken goal and the output share alike) makes you a **Regional power**, **Great power**, **Superpower** and **Hegemon**. Each tier opens or widens: your own interest rate, how much you can print, bond terms, war bonds, directed credit, a wealth fund, national projects, state enterprises, law slots (3 to 7), how far schools can specialize, the military academy, arms exports, occupation policies, capitals, bigger World Bank loans and the World Council. You keep a tier until you fall a tenth below its line. The top bar names your tier; the Empire desk shows what the next one adds.
- **People.** Your workers are basic workers, skilled workers, engineers, doctors and scientists. **Schools** (spending 0 to 4, a stage and a focus) decide the mix and how educated people are; a focus **specializes** the people over two years. Factories run on engineers, universities on scientists, hospitals on doctors: the People tab shows how well each is staffed. Only people with **jobs** pay tax. **Health** (spending, hospitals, homes, medicine) decides life expectancy, growth and calm. **Welfare** (pensions, jobless support, housing) buys stability and an easier election. People **migrate** toward a better life, as your immigration policy allows. **Epidemics** start where health is poor, cross borders and ease with doctors, vaccines and a quarantine. The People tab is described in full [below](#caring-for-the-people-and-building-for-the-future).
- **Money.** From a Regional power you run a **central bank**: a higher rate cools prices, a lower one warms them. **Printing** money gives cash now and inflation later; it never pays for itself. A **currency** moves with rates, inflation, credit and war. **Bonds** run 1, 3 or 5 years at the rate fixed when sold, and are rolled over or repaid when due. **War bonds**, **directed credit** and a **wealth fund** open as you grow; the largest economy of a Superpower or Hegemon holds the **reserve currency**. Dividends follow the world interest rate, investments run down as they pay, and a corruption sweep finds less the sooner it follows the last, so none of it is free money.
- **Industry.** **Public works** spending sets trade, factories, wall repairs and building prices. Seventeen **national projects** take months and engineers, and each can be built to three levels. The state can buy or **seize** a business sector. How big a sector can grow depends on the skills of your people. Projects are described in full [below](#caring-for-the-people-and-building-for-the-future).
- **Empire.** Bonuses from many countries add up with diminishing returns, so a vast empire no longer grows with the square of its size. Your officials can run only so many countries (**administration**); beyond it, stability and output fall. Civic halls, governors, **capitals and regional capitals** help. **Occupation policy** sets how conquered land settles. A Superpower can put **World Council** resolutions to a weighted vote.
- **Puppet states.** A crushing win can make the loser a **puppet** (Medium and Hard rivals do it too when they win clearly, to a country worth ruling, and post troops in a shaky puppet beside them; Easy never does). A winner at war to free a puppet can instead set it **free** at the peace table (independent, no tribute, and grateful). It keeps its land, pays you tribute and (while loyal) fights beside you. The **Puppets** tab of the Empire desk (it shows once you have one) is its control card: once a month, for influence, you set its **tax** (up to 45%), **draft**, one of six **laws** (martial law, conscription, free trade, austerity, rationing, press control), its welfare **spending**, the **form of its tribute** (a share of its money, oil, steel or soldiers) and send **aid**. Each puppet has a **loyalty** (0 to 100) that moves every month by the pressure of your choices (heavy taxes, draft, harsh laws and a heavy tribute lower it; aid, your investment in its businesses, a garrison of your troops on its border and fair taxes raise it) and is pulled back toward 60, so it settles where those balance and loyalty above 60 has to be paid for again every month. The card shows the price, the loyalty it settles at and the tribute you would get before you confirm. A loyal puppet (75+) pays up to 1.2 times its tribute and counts 60% of its countries toward your tier, annexing it costs half; a wavering one (under 35) loses stability, under 25 it rebels, refuses your wars and counts 35% (annexing it costs up to half as much again and its land settles slowly), and the world gets a reason for war to free it; under 15 it can break free. All your puppets together can pay at most a quarter of your own income (the rest at half), so puppets are worth having but never worth more than conquering the land. A puppet's own bot no longer sets the taxes, draft, laws and spending you control; in an online game a puppet run by another player keeps its own government. A puppet made before this existed keeps its flat 12% until you first rule it. Rules are in `engine/puppets.ts`, every number in `data/puppets.ts`, the bots' play in `ai/puppets.ts` (Easy leaves puppets alone, Medium sets a moderate rule, Hard balances loyalty against tribute).
- **Military and research.** An **academy** (levels 1 to 3) sends out drilled recruits and sharpens combined arms. Doctors return part of the wounded infantry to the ranks. Commanders have traits (mountains, logistics, blitz, air, defense). **Arms exports** earn money at a cost in goodwill. Spare research buys **future programs** that raise output, health or weapons.
- **Twelve new events** (bank run, inflation riot, brain drain, doctors' strike, medical breakthrough, collapsed bridge, housing boom, pension crisis, refugees, currency attack, council deadlock, project overruns) bring these systems into your decisions.
- Older saves load: the old budget becomes spending levels, the healthcare law becomes health spending, and debt becomes a bond.

## Factions

A **faction** is a company that countries own together. Open the **Faction** desk (`F`), found one (15 influence) and invite friends. People and rivals can share one faction: online, several humans can sit in the same one and vote on the same wars.

- **The pool.** Members put in money, oil, steel, goods, food and soldiers (by unit type). Each member's **ownership share** follows what it put in, valued at fixed prices (money 1, oil 2, steel 1.8, goods 1.1, food 1.3, and soldiers at what they cost to raise), after any losses the pool has taken. The pool pays its army's upkeep and buys its fuel and food on the market; if it cannot, members are charged by share, and what stays unpaid deserts. Pooled soldiers never defend their home country, so a nation fighting a war the faction is not in cannot pool soldiers.
- **The charter**, chosen at founding: votes are *one member one vote* or *weighted by share*; conquered land is *split by share* or goes *to the proposer*; leaving *refunds* (your claim in kind, less 10%) or *forfeits*.
- **Voting.** Anyone puts a country to the vote with the **Propose country** list (searchable, ranked like the new-game list, each row showing why a country cannot be chosen). Members vote **Yes** or **No**; the count updates as you queue your vote, and a notice, a badge on the nav rail and an advisor item say when a vote needs you. Votes are orders, so they wait for the month's end (on the 1st in a live game), and a vote put forward in one month is decided from the next. It **passes** as soon as more than half the weight says yes, **fails** on a tie or when half says no, and when its two months run out it is decided by the votes cast, if at least half the weight spoke. Humans vote by order and rivals by what they think of the target, whether they have a reason for war and whether the faction looks strong enough; Easy rivals never join in ganging up on anyone. A weighted vote counts each member's weight on the day the proposal was put forward, so money paid in afterwards cannot buy it. Medium and Hard rivals found factions, pay in, put wars to the vote and offer to sell fuel or rations to the pool at the market price (and vote down a contract that pays above it).
- **The joint war.** A passed vote declares the war through the usual rules (a reason costs 10 influence, none costs 60 and brings the world's distrust, paid by the proposer unless a member with a reason declares it), every member that is free joins, and each month the pooled army attacks the weakest reachable country of the target **as one army**, with the lead member's training, weapons and technology, through the same combat code as a national attack. In a turn game the attacks (at most two a month) are fought at once; in a live game the army sets out on the 1st (the day the vote passed), marches as a real army on the globe under the lead member's name, fights on arrival and comes home, one army at a time; it carries a copy of the pool, so its losses are charged to the pool and no member's own army is touched, and no single member can recall it. Captured countries are given out by ownership share (or to the proposer), and so is the treasure that comes with a fallen nation.
- **No cheating the pool.** Gifts are checked against what you have, counting orders already queued. What you put in is locked for two months and you take back only what you put in, in kind (money for money), shrunk by the same share as everyone's if the pool lost any; soldiers stay while the faction is at war; no one can take another's claim. Money cannot be created, lost or moved between members by contributing and withdrawing, whatever the order.
- **Contracts.** Members can put a supply contract to the vote (send the pool oil or steel every month, optionally for payment, with collateral). Medium and Hard rivals back a standing supply they ask a friend for with a small collateral; Easy rivals never do. Between any two nations a trade deal becomes a **live contract**: a start up to six months away, collateral held until it ends, and *they join every war I fight*. It runs by itself each month. Breaking it costs the breaker the collateral, reputation, and gives the other side a reason for war.

## The world answers a rising power

As you grow, the rest of the world takes notice. A **threat** (0 to 100) measures how alarming you are: your empire tier, your share of the world's countries, people and output, and your reputation for aggression. The **tier decides how much of the threat turns into action**: a Regional power barely stirs anyone, a Great power draws partners together, a Superpower and a Hegemon unite the world. It is always limited by the difficulty you chose.

- **Easy** rivals never band together against you, whatever you become.
- **Medium** rivals form alliances and blocs against a growing power (two nations that both border it or fight it ally readily, and a bot looks for such a partner), and join a war against it to defend a friend.
- **Hard** rivals do both, and also press the war of an ally against you, and **plan pre-emptive joint strikes** once you are a Great power: several rivals that border you (and the allies who can reach you) agree to declare war and attack your weakest border together, in the same month. In a live game every attack of the strike leaves on the same day. The plan is announced in the news, in your advisor and in the **World reaction** tab of the Empire desk with at least two months of warning, and each member has a reason for war on paper and a treaty with the strongest. Rivals rest for 14 months (7 when the world is at its most frightened) before planning again.
- The **World reaction** tab shows your threat and where it comes from, which blocs and neighbors are drawing together against you, what is coming, and what you can do about it: pacts with the neighbors in the plan, a World Council recognition that lowers your reputation, aid and mediation in crises (Diplomacy desk), and strengthening the borders the plan names.

## Superpower weapons: nuclear strikes

A **Superpower** (25 countries) with Hypersonic Missiles can build **nuclear warheads** in the War Room: one at a time, each costing 450 money, 40 steel and 60 research and four months to build, up to four in store and a little upkeep for each. A warhead can be fired at a **country of a nation you are at war with** (it needs a reason for war like any other, and a country of yours with a factory to launch from; its missile reaches anywhere on Earth).

- **Effect**: the country's buildings, factories and Defense Line fall to almost nothing, about two thirds of its people die, its factories stop for two years and its output stays crippled (a tenth of normal, healing over four years), and rebels rise. The nation's army is destroyed too (97% if it is the capital or a nation of three countries or fewer, 60% if it is part of a larger one). An Interceptor Shield stops only half its own rating (a level 3 shield stops a quarter). The country is left open: any attack takes it, and a nation with nothing else can fall.
- **Consequences**: your reputation for aggression rises by 60; every nation's opinion of you drops by 50 and recovers over about eleven years; the victim's by 60; **every nation that is not your ally sanctions you for two years**; the world's tension spikes; and **the two strongest alliance blocs (or the two strongest nations, if there are fewer blocs) declare war on you**, each member within its own war limit (Easy rivals send only the first). Another human is never dragged in. A confirmation dialog spells all of this out and asks you to tick a box before the button works. Missile-hunter fighters (aircraft development) catch a warhead only 30% as well as a missile, and stealth takes their share back.
- **Live mode**: the warhead flies for five days as a visible arc over the globe and the target's owner gets alerts; making peace before it lands calls it off. In a turn game it lands when the month ends.
- **Rivals**: only **Hard** rivals that are themselves Superpowers build or fire them, only when **desperate** (a broken capital or a force under 40% of the foe's) or **provoked** (struck with missiles or a bomb), no more than once in three years each, and never within eighteen months of any bomb anywhere. Your advisor warns you of a nuclear enemy.
## Active military and borders

Every nation keeps forces on station, and every pair of neighbors has a border with a tension of its own.

- **Deployment** (Military, *Deployment*). Post shares of your forces on station: a **coastal patrol** and a **submarine screen** (shares of your warships), an **air patrol** (a share of fighters and drones) and a **border garrison** for each neighbor you share a land border with (shares of your infantry, tanks, artillery and robots). What is not posted is the **reserve**. Rivals do the same: Easy rivals post a token garrison and patrol, Medium rivals place garrisons by threat and add a submarine screen where the sea is hostile, and Hard rivals keep the most on station and best placed. A nation that has never posted forces (a player who never opens the tab) has no posture: no bonus and no weak spot, exactly as the game always played.
  - **Rules.** Posted forces cannot attack: only the reserve can be sent (the War Room says how much that is, and shares are of the reserve). They still cost their upkeep, food and oil. They still defend, but where they stand matters: a garrison fights at +35% on its own border and at half strength on any other front, and patrols and the air screen fight at +35% against a landing from the sea or air. Moving forces takes a month: a posture can change once a month. A posted fleet cannot carry an invasion.
  - **Garrisons** add Defense Line to the countries along their border (the garrison shared among them, at most 3 points), and a border they guard well (about eight tenths of a division for each country along it) cannot be surprised: in the first months of a war an attack across a border nobody guards fights up to 25% harder. A nation with a posture and an unguarded border has a weak spot, and the report says when it was used.
  - **Patrols** meet a landing that crosses water or air and sink part of it before it comes ashore (the more patrol per division landed, up to 30%): coastal patrols against a crossing over a sea lane, the submarine screen against a fleet on the open ocean, air patrols against a landing from the air. Air patrols also shoot down part of a cruise or tactical missile salvo. The battle report and the news say what the patrols sank, in a live game your patrols sight a landing force the day it sets out (with the days to arrival and the share they expect to sink), and a live interception shows a ring on the globe and a notification.
  - **Threat watch** lists every rival at war with you, or in an incident with you, that can reach you today, by the way it would come, and says how well that way is guarded. Its numbers are exact when you have intelligence on the rival and a rounded guess when you do not; the advisor puts a danger at the top of the Briefing. The same guess-or-exact rule covers what rivals keep on station: allies, enemies at war, puppets and nations you have spied on show exact numbers, anyone else only a rounded guess that holds for a year (and, on a border, only the part facing you). *Auto-station* fills in the posture a careful government would keep.
- **Borders.** Every pair of neighbors, by land or across a sea lane, has a border with a **tension** from 0 to 100 and a state: **open**, **fortified** (either side guards it well), **closed** (one side closed it) or **disputed** (at war, or a tension of 60 or more). Tension is worked out from the game as it stands: neighbors by land (10) or sea (5), war (+60), recent incidents (+24 each, fading 4 a month), troops massed on the border (up to +18), a closed border (+10), a grievance (+8), less for a pact (-20), an alliance (-35), a puppet and its master (-30) and trade partners (-8). The country panel shows the border with the country you clicked, and the Deployment tab lists every border with its parts and a button to close or reopen it (3 influence to close). A closed border halves provocations and adds cover, but it adds tension and the neighbor remembers.
  - **Incidents.** Neighbors that dislike each other clash now and then, as before, and now troops massed on a border make a clash likelier and let neighbors who like each other clash too. A border garrison that guards its border well turns a provocation back before it becomes an incident. An incident gives the wronged nation a reason for war (the same *Border incident* reason as before, valid for 8 months), adds heat to the border and is remembered in its tension. Across the sea an intruder is only noticed by a nation that watches its waters with patrols.
  - **The Borders map** (key `7`) draws every land border in the color of its tension (green, amber, red; thicker where it is guarded, dashed where it is closed), the sea lanes you share with other nations and the strategic sea chokepoints, and puts small **badges** on the globe for the forces on station you can see: a ship (coastal patrol), a submarine, a plane (air patrol) and a shield (a garrison on a border), each with its divisions. Badges show in every map mode; in the Borders map a rival's garrison facing you also shows as a guess when you have no intelligence on it.

## Caring for the people, and building for the future

Money is only worth what you do with it. Two Economy tabs are where it goes.

### People

The **People** tab has seven sections: *Overview*, *Health*, *Learning*, *Work*, *Family*, *Safety* and *Campaigns*.

- **Why stability is heading where it is.** The Overview lists every cause of stability with its points: taxes, hunger, empty shops, debt, war weariness, government and laws, technology, rebels, stretched officials, health and welfare, prices, epidemics, conquered land, immigration and your programs. Add them to the starting calm and you get where stability is heading, so you know which lever to pull.
- **22 programs in five groups.** Health and care (community clinics, clean water, mental health, elder care), Learning (school meals, scholarships, apprenticeships, libraries), Work (minimum wage, a public jobs guarantee, small-business loans, workers' rights), Family and homes (child benefit, parental leave, food assistance, public transport) and Safety and unity (community policing, courts and anti-corruption, integration, festivals, public broadcasting, state surveillance). Each is **Off, Pilot, National or Universal** (Universal needs a Regional power).
  - Raising a program costs **influence** for each step, then **money every month** by its level and the size of your workforce. Its effect **phases in over a few months** and fades out the same way. **Cutting** one costs stability at once, because people do not forgive losing what they were given. A program can change once a month.
  - Programs feed the real numbers: health, schooling, jobs, births, the stability needed to win an election, how likely rebels are to rise, how fast conquered land settles, epidemics, how hard shortages bite, and how many countries your officials can run. Several carry a real trade-off: minimum wage slows business, state surveillance drives scientists away and democracies punish it, workers' rights slow factories, parental leave costs output.
  - Stability from care has a **ceiling** (18 points at the very most, shared by programs, national projects, campaigns and the cultural centres in your countries), so the first programs help most and nobody buys their way to perfect calm.
  - **Calm costs; growth pays.** Measured by simulating five years, the programs for growth (courts, public transport, small-business loans, apprenticeships) earn back roughly two thirds to nine tenths of their cost in income, plus research and jobs. Programs for calm are a pure cost.
- **7 campaigns.** A national celebration, a vaccination drive, a literacy campaign, a job fair, a unity tour of the provinces, a food bank drive and a baby bonus. Each is a one-off bill (by the size of your country) for a burst of effect, with a long wait before the next.

### Projects

The **Projects** tab has two views.

- **National.** Seventeen projects, grouped by the size of empire that opens them. The first seven are rail, power grid, deep-water ports, science park, medical research centre, digital government and a space launch complex. The new ten are a clean water network, a national stadium, national broadband, a strategic grain reserve, an open university, a World Expo (every nation thinks better of its host), a national AI lab, a fusion power plant, a planned capital city and a Mars mission.
  - Every project can be built to **three levels**. Each level costs and takes more, and is worth more but not in proportion, so the first level is the best deal. The levels you have keep working while the next is built. Level 3 needs at least a Regional power. Upkeep is a share of everything put in.
  - You can build 2, 3, 4, 5 or 6 at once as a Nation, Regional power, Great power, Superpower or Hegemon. **Engineers set the pace**, so building many at once slows them all.
  - **Rush** a project: pay four months of its bill to bring it two months closer (fewer while engineers are scarce: extra crews cannot make engineers), once a month for each project.
- **In your countries.** Each country you hold can have its own projects, built up to three levels, and they **stay with the land** if it is conquered (a half-built one is dropped). There are nine: an industrial park, a tech hub (needs a university), irrigation, a hydroelectric dam (needs mountains or forest), a tourist resort (needs a coast), a cultural centre (conquered land settles faster, and rebels rarer), a border fortress, a special economic zone (needs a port) and a mine expansion (needs deposits). A bigger country costs more to build in and gives more back, so the return is much the same wherever you build. You can build 2, 3, 5, 7 or 10 at once by empire size.

### The space program

The **Space** tab (Economy) is a long investment in reaching other worlds. It needs Fifth-Generation Fighters, and has five sections: *Agency*, *Rockets*, *Missions*, *Labs* and *Colonies*.

- **Agency.** Hire **engineers** and **astronauts**: they come out of the same specialists your factories and universities use (the cap follows your workforce, schooling and engineering focus), they are paid every month, and a big agency takes engineers from the factories. Fly missions to earn **experience**, which unlocks harder missions and makes every launch safer. A **launch site** (three levels) gives places for rockets and flights; the national *space launch complex* project adds a place, lowers rocket prices and lets the biggest pad be built. The whole program is one line in your Budget (*Space program*), and its mines are another (*Space mining*).
- **Rockets.** Five classes: sounding, orbital, heavy-lift, deep-space and interplanetary. Each is **developed** once (months of engineers' time; two more levels make it more reliable) and then **built** one at a time (money, steel, months). A launch uses its rocket up.
- **Missions.** A ladder of nineteen missions with long, phased voyages (build, launch, travel, arrive; the Mars landing takes about three years). A seeded roll decides the launch and another the arrival; a failure loses the rocket and payload, costs standing, can kill astronauts, and gives a little experience. Satellites give real bonuses (communications: trade and research; weather: calm and less wasted food; reconnaissance: intelligence on every enemy, and it works better with the Orbital Network). Every mission can be flown **in-house** (payload paid over the build, a rocket from your hangar, talent and experience needed, builds experience) or **outsourced** to a private company: a fixed price at a premium over the in-house cost, a faster build, no experience gained or needed, and the company's own record. Companies are AI entities whose price, record and heaviest rocket improve as the world's space industry grows, and the money you pay lifts the technology business of the nation they are based in (a sixth of it stays at home with a firm of your own).
- **Labs and samples.** Probes, landers and crews bring home samples (lunar regolith, Martian soil, asteroid metal, ice cores, exotica). Labs (three levels) turn samples, research and money into **discoveries**, a data-driven tree: cheaper and more reliable rockets, faster voyages, closed-loop life support, in-situ resource use and richer mines, and spillovers at home (output, factories, trade, medicine, calm, even Modern Surgery or Nanotechnology).
- **Colonies and mining.** Land on a world, then **claim** it (influence; nobody else may build there, and a claim with no outpost on it lapses after five years; Hard rivals race for the same worlds), then fly the cargo mission that founds an outpost. Outposts grow into stations and colonies (research, calm) and carry **mines** that take two to three years to build and need experience and the in-situ discovery: metals (steel), rare metals (money), water and volatiles (oil, cheaper upkeep) and helium-3 (money, grid power, and a bonus to a national fusion plant). Discoveries make mines richer up to 50% in all. Everything is paid for in upkeep, and an outpost the treasury cannot supply goes quiet and shrinks. Measured by simulating with events off, every mine together with its outpost (cargo flight included) pays back in money, steel and oil in between about eight and fourteen years, never sooner; that is before the cost of the rockets, labs and staff that got you there, so the program is an investment in reach and bonuses, never free money.
- Medium rivals run a modest program (a launch site, satellites); Hard rivals climb the whole ladder and claim worlds. They spend only while their income stays in the black.

## The War Room: your force and the odds

- **Choose your force unit by unit.** The attack order has an optional `units` (`Partial<UnitCounts>`): exactly how many divisions of each kind to send. It replaces the presets and the share (`commit` and `share` still work and old orders are untouched). It is checked (`unitsProblem` in `src/engine/deploy.ts`): no negative or non-number counts, no more than is **free** (the reserve after forces on station, units already named this month and orders already queued), at least half a division, and never missiles (`missiles` names those). In a turn game the soldiers stay in the military after a fight, so a unit sent by name is spent for the month (`Military.sent`, cleared when the turn ends); in a live game the army carries exactly those units and a 10% home guard stays.
- **The chance to win is the real fight.** `src/engine/forecast.ts` plays the real `resolveBattle` many times on evenly spread (Kronecker) dice, so a number is the same on every click, moves smoothly as you change a stepper, and costs about 8 ms. `forecast.test.ts` plays the same attacks as long simulations of `resolveAttack` itself on copies of the state: 40 varied scenarios, mean error about a point, worst under 5. Beside the number: a range, the Defense Line push, the chance the country falls, losses by kind, and a list of what decides it (strength against defence, terrain, line, stance, combined arms, supply and oil, training and technology, garrison, air cover, missiles, defender reinforcement, landing penalty, surprise), each with a tooltip.
- **Without AI Battle Command** you see only a rough band (`roughOdds`): a label and a rounded guess that always holds the real chance but never pins it down, wider the less you can see (a war front, spies, an Orbital Network).
- **AI Battle Command** (`src/engine/commander.ts`, panel in `src/ui/CommandCenter.tsx`): level 1 exact odds with range and breakdown, and your targets ranked (win, losses, gains, risks); level 2 also the best stance and written advice (what to do first, the weakest front, when to wait, supply and weariness warnings, what an attack leaves open at home); level 3 *Plan this attack for me* fills the steppers from a bounded, deterministic search over compositions for a goal (best chance to win, fewest losses, fastest capture, keep home safe), a what-if table against your own plan, and a standing **auto-command**: each month it plans attacks within your limits (most attacks, share kept home, least chance to win) and puts them in the order tray in green. Click one to cancel it before **Next turn**. Auto-command is for turn games (not live or online).
- **Combat rules, tuned as a stats game** (see `docs/WAR_STATUS.md` for the tables): a battle has a *fortune of the day* (`COMBAT.luck`) so odds are real probabilities instead of a switch; every unit has a `toughness` (how much damage a division soaks up: infantry 1, tanks 3, robots 4.5) so a dearer unit is not always beaten by three cheap ones; Siege no longer beats Assault at everything. `combatMatrix.sweep.ts` prints the tables, `combatMatrix.test.ts` pins them.

## Missiles

Missiles are weapons that need no army. A nation can have five kinds, **every one of which kills people and cuts the Defense Line**; they differ in how much, and in what else they do. Each one fired is used up:

| Kind | Comes from | Reach | Flight (live) | People per missile | Line per missile | Its character |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cruise | missile program, level 1 | 3500 km | 2 days | 0.08% | 0.25 | cheapest line damage per money; stops factories (3 needed); burns fuel and airfields; a shield stops the most of these |
| Tactical | program, level 2 | 2500 km | 1 day | 0.05% | 0.12 | kills a slice of the army; the best counterforce and airbase weapon; spares civilians most |
| Hypersonic | the Hypersonic Missiles technology and the training queue | 6000 km | 1 day | 0.15% | 0.6 | the fastest and hardest to stop; cracks the line; stops factories two months |
| Ballistic | program, level 3 | 9000 km | 3 days | 0.4% | 1.0 | the most dead, destroys buildings, draws 1.5 interceptor rounds; the world will not forgive it |
| Decoy | program, level 1 | 3000 km | 1 day | 0.01% | 0.02 | costs 1.5 money and a third of a program slot; its job is to make a shield spend rounds |

Every number is data in `src/data/missiles.ts` (people, line, urban multiplier, stability, war-crime reputation, counterforce, fuel, airfields, interceptor drain, tension) and `src/data/missileWar.ts`. Cities make a strike deadlier (`urban`), the dead cost the struck nation stability and war weariness, earn the attacker a reputation scaled by the kind and the world's tension, come off the country's people (so off the economy) and count toward the war score. Nuclear weapons stay the extreme and keep their own rules.

- **The missile program** (Military, Systems) has three levels. Each costs money and research once, opens the next kind and widens how many missiles the program can build in a month (5, 10, 18). The card now opens on **Next steps**: concrete things to press (build, deploy fighters, launch, scramble, restock rounds, set a doctrine, offer a ceasefire).
- **Firing** is in the **Missiles** section of the War Room: every kind you hold, with stock, range, days, what it does, an **aim** (the country, or its launch sites) and a **forecast** (the chance to get through and the expected dead, line, store destroyed). Fire 1, or a Custom number. In a live game the salvo flies and lands after its kind's days; in a turn game it resolves when the month ends.
- **An attack on land never carries missiles by itself.**

### The missile war

Military, **Missile War** is the desk for it (`src/ui/MissileWar.tsx`, rules in `src/engine/missileWar.ts`, `missileDoctrine.ts`, `missileForecast.ts`).

- **Interceptors run out.** A shield holds 8 rounds a level, a battery 4 more. Every missile that arrives spends `drain` rounds (ballistic 1.5) and the shield's stopping power falls with the share of the salvo it has rounds for, so a big salvo or a wave of **decoys** saturates it. Half the store comes back each month; rounds can be bought. With a full store, no battery and no radar the old shield-and-hunters rule is reproduced exactly (tested).
- **Early-warning radar** (3 levels; finished *radar network* and *integrated air shield* aircraft developments count as airborne early warning) shows a salvo the day it leaves and cues the rounds, **air-defence batteries** protect one country, **mobile launchers** hide missiles from counterforce, **fighters** at home or on air patrol shoot missiles down and, in a live game, can be **scrambled** against one specific salvo.
- **Counterforce:** aim a strike at a launch site (a country with a factory). It does 35% of the harm to people and the line, but destroys a share of the enemy's missiles in store (not warheads) and shuts his launch site for a few months. Mobile launchers hide from it unless a **recon** flight found them.
- **Bomber raids** (an order with its own forecast): strike aircraft fly out with fighter escorts and meet the defender's fighters and batteries; losses, the chance each division gets home and the harm done are shown first. Stealth and escorts bring more home.
- **Doctrine:** *Hold fire*, *Retaliate* (fires back once at the launch site that hit you; never answers an answer) and *Launch on warning* (live, needs radar; fires while the salvo is in the air, with a small monthly **false alarm** risk that grows with tension and shrinks with radar, and only ever at a nation you are already at war with).
- **Escalation ladder:** every strike that gets through raises the world's tension (by kind; counterforce 60% as much). Calm, Skirmish, Missile war, Strategic exchange, Nuclear brink. It fades each month, faster when nobody fires, and counts toward the world tension. High rungs make the world judge civilian deaths harder, and rivals stop opening salvos at the brink.
- **Missile ceasefire:** offer one to an AI rival. It accepts when the tension is high (10 for Easy, 25 Normal, 40 Hard) or it has lost more than it dealt. It stops missiles, raids and warheads between the two for six months and cools the world; armies keep fighting. Lifting it costs reputation.
- **Every salvo has a report** (`GameState.salvos`, last 40): launched, stopped by the shield, batteries, fighters, patrols, got through, dead, line lost, store destroyed, site shut, aircraft lost. The globe draws it (a trail per kind, launch flash, interception burst with debris, impact ring and shake scaled by the kind, craters and smoke that fade over days, a radar sweep, aircraft sprites, a pulsing screen edge while a salvo is incoming; one ring and no motion with reduced motion; the colour-blind palette has its own trail colours and trails differ in height and thickness too).
- **Rivals:** Easy rivals do none of it. Normal rivals keep a retaliation doctrine, some radar and rounds, and raid now and then. Hard rivals run counterforce (decoys first against a full shield), raids on launchers, recon against mobile launchers, batteries, launch on warning once their radar is good, and offer ceasefires as the tension climbs.

See [docs/MISSILE_WAR_STATUS.md](docs/MISSILE_WAR_STATUS.md) for what is done, the numbers and what is not.

## Aircraft development

The **Develop** button on Military, Forces opens a large screen with a tree of sixteen aircraft developments in four branches. It answers missiles and drones, and gives bombers and fighters new abilities.

- **Interceptors**: radar network, long-range air-to-air missiles, **missile-hunter fighters** and an integrated air shield. Fighters kept at home shoot down a share of every incoming missile salvo and drone swarm: it works with the Interceptor Shield (the shield stops its share, then the hunters their share of what is left), cruise missiles are easiest to catch and ballistic warheads hardest, and a wing of three fighters at home covers everything (fewer cover less). In `missiles.ts` this is `getsThrough(shield, kind, air)`; the same cover blunts hypersonic batteries in an attack and drones and missiles in a battle.
- **Stealth and electronic war**: radar-absorbing coatings, stealth bombers, electronic warfare pods and a blackout doctrine. Bombers keep more of their strength when the enemy rules the air; enemy shields and hunters stop less of your missiles, drones and bombers; enemy hunters and fighters are blinded.
- **Range and payload**: drop tanks, aerial refuelling and a strategic bomber wing push airbase reach beyond 7000 km (up to 13000 km), landings by air lose less of their penalty, and attacks with many bombers push the Defense Line back harder.
- **Unmanned wingmen**: wingmen, swarm autonomy, loyal wingman fighters and an autonomous air force. Stronger drones that survive enemy fighters, better fighters, and faster development.

Each development has a cost in research (paid the day it starts), money (paid month by month while it is worked on, the same total at any pace), months of work (engineers set the pace, like national projects), earlier developments, a technology and an empire tier (Nation, Regional power, Great power, Superpower). A nation develops one thing at a time, two from a Great power; pausing one frees its slot and its monthly bill. A card shows what it changes on the unit stat sheet (attack and defense before and after) and why it is locked. The numbers on the screen are the numbers battles use: developments add to the combat modifiers (`combatMods`) that the stat sheet, battles, the forecast and the AI all read. Hard bots at war, or facing missiles, develop their aircraft (interceptors first when missiles threaten). Everything is data in `src/data/aircraft.ts` and pure rules in `src/engine/aircraft.ts`; a nation that never develops anything has no `aircraft` field and plays exactly as before.

## Live mode

Pick **Live** on the start screen and the same game runs on a clock: a month takes about a minute at normal speed (a day every two seconds), with pause, 2x and 4x. A new game starts paused; **Space** plays or pauses, **1**, **2** and **3** set the speed. Everything else works as in Turns: the economy, research, diplomacy, agencies, crises, stocks, Frontier systems, governors and bulk upgrades.

- **Orders.** Upgrades, training, trades, shares and war work at once. Research, taxes, the draft and offers to other nations wait for the 1st of the next month, and the Orders bar says when. Answering an offer, such as accepting peace, counts at once.
- **Armies.** An attack in the War Room sends an army out of your military. It leaves from a border country, a port or an airbase, marches for days (by land, across a sea lane, by fleet or by air: the War Room shows the days and the dates), fights with the usual combat rules when it arrives, stays a day, and marches home; the survivors rejoin your military. Units that are away do not defend your country, but still cost upkeep. Recall an army while it is on its way out, or turn it back by making peace.
- **On the globe.** Each army is a badge in its nation's color (a plane, ship, tank or infantry mark). Yours have a white outline and a dashed blue route; enemy armies marching on you are outlined in red with a red route, a pulsing ring over the country and an alert in the top bar with the days left. Battles show a pin with the Defense Line draining and the result; missiles fly as arcs in their kind's color and land after that kind's days (see [Missiles](#missiles)); an orbital strike hits at once. Missiles in the air toward you show a purple alert in the top bar, "Missiles incoming from X: N days".
- **The AI** plans once a month as in Turns, and its attacks leave on days of the month, so you see them coming.
- **What stops the clock:** pausing, a decision (an event), a battle window (opt in with Settings > Pause on my battles), the monthly report if you chose that, the War Room, and the help window. The clock never runs in the background of a hidden tab. Live games are single player; playing with friends online uses Turns.

## Play with friends (1 to 4 players)

On the main menu press **Play with friends online**, type a name, and **Create a room**. You get a five-letter code. Friends press the same button, choose **Join a room** and type it. In the lobby everyone picks a nation, and the host sets the rules: rival skill (Easy, Medium, Hard), how you win (hold the whole world, or a short, standard or long game of countries taken, people held or share of the world's output), and how long each month lasts (no timer, 1, 1.5, 2 or 3 minutes). Nations nobody picks are run by the AI, and AI nations still ally, trade and fight each other.

- **Everyone plans the same month at once.** Press **Ready** when you are done. The month moves on when every player is ready, or when the timer ends. Press the button again to keep planning. Players who are away are skipped, and their nation is run by the AI until they return.
- **Attacks wait for the end of the month online.** In a one-player game an attack plays out the moment you order it. Online, it joins your plan and is resolved at month end in a seeded order, so nobody wins by clicking faster. Builds, trades and training still happen at once.
- **Surprise decisions come to each player separately** and never hold the month. Choose when you like; the answer travels with your plan. If you do not choose, your ministers take the first option when the month ends.
- The first player to reach the goal wins, and the end screen names the winner and shows every player's countries, people and strength. The host can offer a **Rematch**. A player whose nation falls keeps watching; the game ends when someone wins or every player has fallen.
- Open **Players and chat** under the Ready button to see who is ready (the Ready box says who the month is waiting on), to talk, and (as host) to remove a player. Each player has their own color. Offers between players wait in the other player's inbox until the next month begins, and you get a note when one arrives.
- **If the host presses Leave or reloads, the next player takes over at once**, in the lobby or in a game. If the host's tab simply closes or their connection dies, the others notice within about 12 seconds and the next player takes over. A screen whose own connection has dropped says **Reconnecting** and never crowns itself host; when the line returns it asks for the game and sends its plan again.
- **Saved games.** After every month each player's device keeps the room (squeezed to about 60 KB, the last three rooms). If everyone closes their tab, any player opens it again from **Saved games** under Play with friends, choosing which nation to take, and the others join with the same code and take their seats back. A player on a new device takes the seat of someone who is away the same way.
- There is no undo online, since your orders are already on their way to the host. Offline, **Undo** takes back plain orders but never a missile salvo or warhead, an orbital strike, an attack fought at once in a turn game, or an offer a rival answered on the spot in a live game: those cannot be taken back (and they cut off the steps before them), so a result can never be peeked at and retried.

### Connecting players

The room code is shared through [Supabase Realtime](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/realtime) broadcast. No app server, database tables or accounts are needed, only a project's URL and public publishable key, which the browser reads at build time:

1. Create a free project at supabase.com. Realtime broadcast is on by default.
2. Copy **Project URL** and the **publishable key** from *Project Settings, API*.
3. For local use put them in `.env.local` (see `.env.example`). For a deployed site set `VITE_SUPABASE_URL` and `VITE_SUPABASE_PUBLISHABLE_KEY` where the site is built, then rebuild and republish. The app also accepts `NEXT_PUBLIC_SUPABASE_URL` and `NEXT_PUBLIC_SUPABASE_PUBLISHABLE_KEY`. Vite bakes these public values into the browser bundle.

```bash
cp .env.example .env.local   # then edit the two values
npm run dev
```

Without them the game still runs, and **Play with friends** works in a practice mode between tabs of the same browser (open the page twice). It cannot connect different computers.

How it works: the host's browser runs the same pure engine as a one-player game and is the referee. Other players send their plan for the month to the host, who checks every order against that player's own nation, resolves the month, and sends the new game back (compressed, in pieces under 50 KB). Everyone's game is therefore identical. The room is a trust-your-friends design: the host's browser holds the truth, and there is no server-side anti-cheat. Messages are not encrypted beyond what Supabase provides, so do not share the room code with strangers.

Shortcuts: `Enter` next turn, `B` briefing, `E` economy, `M` military, `W` wars, `D` diplomacy, `F` faction, `R` research, `N` news, `A` War Room for the selected country, `?` help, `Esc` close.

## Architecture

```
src/
  engine/      Pure, deterministic simulation (no DOM). resolveTurn(state, map, orders) -> new state
    resolveTurn.ts  one month: month-end orders, training, attacks, rebels, economy, diplomacy, events (runMonth; resolveTurn wraps it)
    live.ts         the live clock: advanceDay(state, map, { monthOrders, planner }) moves a live game by one day, running the month on the last day
    missiles.ts     missile stock, kinds, strike effects per kind, the missile program, who struck whom
    armies.ts       live armies: launch point, march days, recall, the shortest-path position of an army at a given time
    calendar.ts     real month lengths and absolute days for the live clock
    orders.ts       validation and costs. Orders in MONTH_END_ORDERS wait for Next turn; the rest apply at once
    militaryStats.ts  the per-unit stat sheet and military power (battles and the AI read the same numbers)
    deployment.ts   forces on station: the reserve (`freeUnits`), garrisons, patrols, interception, surprise, the posture order
    borders.ts      borders between neighbors: tension, state, incidents, closing a border
    threats.ts      the threat watch: who can reach a nation, and how well that way is guarded
    aircraft.ts     aircraft development: the tree's rules, slots, monthly work, what finished developments add up to (hunters, stealth, reach, payload); data in data/aircraft.ts
    combat.ts / warfare.ts  3-round battles, whole-country capture, garrisons, rebels
    arms.ts / training.ts   weapon levels, training slots and queue
    coalition.ts    the threat a leader poses, the pressure it puts on rivals, joint strikes and the World reaction view
    nuclear.ts      nuclear warheads: the program, strikes and flights, and the world's answer
    economy.ts / diplomacy.ts / espionage.ts / tech.ts / victory.ts
    factions.ts     factions: the pool, ledger and shares, votes, the joint war, contracts with the pool, upkeep (runMonth runs them on the month's schedule)
    society.ts      social programs and campaigns: effects, the care bill, phasing in, orders
    puppets.ts      puppet states: loyalty and its pressure, tribute forms, the master's governPuppet order, annex and tier weights, the call for help
    works.ts        projects built in a country: levels, needs, size-scaled costs, rush
  data/        Tech tree, units, missiles (missiles.ts), buildings, terrain, events, country stats, starting nations, scenarios (scenarios.ts, plain data applied by engine/scenarios.ts), social programs (society.ts), projects (projects.ts, localProjects.ts)
  ai/          Rule-based orders for every nation no human plays (same rules as the player): bot, diplomat, management, opinion, blocs, factions, strategy (postures, forecast-based attacks, peace timing; see docs/STRATEGY.md), deployment (the posture a rival keeps)

    space.ts        the space program: talent, rockets, flights, samples, labs, outposts and mines (spaceEffects.ts is its pure arithmetic)
  net/         Online play: protocol (messages, room codes, compressed snapshots), transport (Supabase Realtime with reconnect status, same-browser fallback), session (lobby, host terms and takeover, seats, ready, timer), saves (rooms kept on each device)
  map/world.ts Natural Earth 110m countries -> regions, adjacency, coastlines, sea lanes
  ui/          React: globe, HUD, drawers, Modal, battle window, diplomacy, tech tree. ui/globe has the army layer (badges, routes, missile arcs) and the border layer (border lines by tension, forces on station); ui/liveClock.ts is the one loop that runs a live game's clock
  store.ts     Zustand store: orders, turn flow, battle queue, autosave (localStorage)
```

The engine is seeded and side-effect free, so the same seed and orders always produce the same world, and a live game's days are deterministic too. Turn games carry none of the live fields, and a pinned test (`turnGolden.test.ts`) keeps a turn month exactly as it was. `GameState.humans` lists the nations people play (one in a one-player game), so a multiplayer game and a single-player game run the same code. Online, the host resolves each month and everyone else receives the result.
